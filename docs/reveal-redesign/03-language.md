# Language That Converts at the Decision Moment — Research for the LEXFIT Plan-Reveal Page

Scope: the exact copy on the plan-reveal page — headline, rail heading/lead, CTA, exit line,
guarantee framing, commitment prompt — evaluated against evidence on CTA verb psychology,
benefit framing, guarantee wording, trust microcopy, quiet-register tone, and personalization.
Brand constraints held throughout: no exclamation marks, no urgency/deadline language, no
weight-loss vocabulary, no second-person health assumptions, no invented statistics or
testimonials, tegeződés.

**A note on method**: this session's web-search budget was exhausted partway through research
(200/200 `WebSearch` calls used session-wide before this track's queries landed). The findings
below come from targeted `WebFetch` reads of primary and aggregator pages instead — real,
individually-verified URLs, but a narrower net than a full search pass would give. Anywhere a
claim rests on an aggregator repeating a named study rather than the primary study itself, it's
flagged. Nothing below is invented; where evidence was too thin to find a live source, that's
stated explicitly rather than filled in.

---

## TL;DR

- **First-person CTA copy ("my") beats second-person ("your") by 10–90% in repeatedly-cited
  A/B tests** (ContentVerve's "Start MY free trial" case is the canonical one) — our CTA
  „Megnézem a tagságot" is already first-person by virtue of Hungarian verb conjugation. Keep
  that property in any rewrite. *(Aggregator-sourced, not the primary test report.)*
- **Inspection verbs ("see/look at") are correct for low-intent, top-of-funnel moments and
  risk under-asking at high-intent decision moments** — evidence on stage-matched CTAs
  (explore → interested → ready) says the reveal page is already past "explore," so a
  commitment-flavored verb is defensible to test against the current inspection verb.
  *(Practitioner-documented pattern, not a controlled study of this exact swap.)*
- **Real subscription/fitness guarantees are condition-first, numeric, and short**: Future.co's
  entire guarantee is "Cancel within 30 days for a full refund." — condition, then payoff, no
  hedging. LEXFIT's guarantee framing line is payoff-first and abstract; it needs a condition-
  first numeric sentence next to it, not instead of it.
- **"Cancel anytime" as unconditional trust microcopy appears on every real example pulled**
  (Basecamp, Kit/ConvertKit, Peloton App) — it's load-bearing enough to be near-universal in
  this category, independent of whether a guarantee also exists.
- **Self-efficacy research (Bandura) says the language that helps a repeat-failure audience is
  effort/strategy attribution ("insufficient effort or approach," not "insufficient ability")**
  — LEXFIT's commitment-prompt hint ("don't pick your best day, pick an average one") already
  does this correctly; it's the strongest line on the page by this standard.
  - **Process praise outperforms outcome/ability praise for resilience after failure**
  (Dweck) — reinforces the same verdict and extends it: avoid outcome language ("you'll
  succeed this time") anywhere near the guarantee or CTA.
- **Personalization at decision moments has real, if generic, lift evidence** — 80% of
  consumers report being more likely to buy from a brand that personalizes, 90% find it
  appealing (Epsilon/GBH, 2017, n=1,000, validated against transactional data) — but this is a
  general marketing stat, not a study of plan-reveal pages specifically. The page currently has
  zero personalized echo of the user's own quiz answers in its decision copy; that's a gap.
- **Outcome/transformation framing beats feature framing next to price** (Copyhackers' SaaS
  pricing audit, opinion-grounded with one cited study) — LEXFIT's rail lead already does this
  ("A heti terved a tiéd... viszi tovább" names what continues, not what you're buying).
- **Hungarian-specific direct-response research is thin online** — the one solid, verifiable
  fact is legal: the EU 14-day right of withdrawal (2011/83/EU, "elállási jog") applies in
  Hungary and is a trust phrase buyers expect to see named, not just implied. Beyond that,
  everything Hungarian-specific below is inference from CEE/DACH-adjacent practice, flagged as
  such.

## 1. CTA verb psychology: inspection vs. possession/commitment verbs

**First person vs. second person** is the best-evidenced single finding in this whole track.
The repeatedly-cited case: ContentVerve changed "Start **your** free 30-day trial" to "Start
**my** free 30-day trial" and saw a reported 90% lift in click-through; the pattern ("my" beats
"your," attributed to "my" implying the visitor already possesses the thing) shows up across
independent CTA-copy roundups as a near-consensus best practice
([wisernotify.com](https://wisernotify.com/blog/call-to-action-stats/),
[unbounce.com](https://unbounce.com/conversion-rate-optimization/cta-buttons-that-convert/),
[kissmetrics.io](https://kissmetrics.io/blog/cta-button-best-practices)). This is aggregator-
reported, not a primary study I could read directly — treat the specific "90%" number as
directional, not precise. **Implication**: LEXFIT's „Megnézem a tagságot" already has this
property for free — Hungarian first-person singular verb conjugation makes every CTA
first-person by default. Any rewrite should preserve a first-person verb form and should NOT
drift into an imperative/second-person construction ("Nézd meg...") which is the Hungarian
equivalent of the weaker "your" framing.

**Inspection vs. possession/commitment verbs** is the harder, more relevant question, and the
evidence is stage-based rather than a direct A/B of verb classes. The clearest documented
pattern: CTA copy should match funnel stage — "See How It Works" for a first-time,
low-commitment visitor; a "Buy Now"-class CTA shown too early produces a worse click-through
than a softer one, and the reverse also holds — under-asking at a high-intent moment leaves
conversion on the table because the CTA doesn't match the reader's actual state
([bima.co.uk](https://bima.co.uk/turning-clicks-into-conversions-the-art-and-science-of-effective-ctas/)).
The LEXFIT reveal page is *not* a first-time, cold visitor — the person just finished a 7-question
quiz, gave their email, and is looking at a plan built for them. That's functionally an
"interested/ready" stage, not "explore." An inspection verb ("I'll look at...") is honest about
what happens on click (it goes to a pricing/options screen, not straight to payment) but it is
also the softest verb available for a reader who has already shown high intent — plausibly
depressing completed clicks into the pricing screen even if it doesn't hurt (or even helps)
raw click-through, because it frames the action as still-exploratory when the reader's own
behavior says otherwise.

**When low-commitment CTA copy helps vs. hurts**: the documented mechanism is intent-matching,
not "soft always wins" or "soft always loses." Progressive-commitment/multi-step flow data
(Typeform's 3.8M-submission benchmark, aggregator-reported via
[HubSpot](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/personalized-calls-to-action-convert-better-data))
shows staged low-commitment openers outperform single-step high-commitment asks *at the start*
of a flow — but LEXFIT's reveal page is not the start of the flow, it's several steps in
(quiz → email → plan reveal → this CTA). Applying "start soft" logic here mismatches the stage.

**Copy implication for the CTA**: test the current „Megnézem a tagságot" against a verb that
names continuation rather than inspection or a fresh beginning — see §2 and §8 for exact
alternatives, and note the through-line: the CTA verb should match the "keep going" reframe the
rail lead already establishes, not undercut it with a softer, exploratory verb.

## 2. Benefit framing at the price

**Outcome/transformation language beats feature/capability language next to price.** Copyhackers'
2026 SaaS pricing-page audit (opinion-grounded, citing one 2018 *Psychological Bulletin* finding
that "goal-oriented messaging" shapes subsequent decisions more than neutral description) shows
real examples swapping feature lists for outcome statements — "Eliminate the information
collection bottleneck" instead of listing form features
([copyhackers.com](https://copyhackers.com/2025/03/saas-pricing-page-checklist/)). LEXFIT's rail
lead already applies this correctly: „A heti terved a tiéd. A LEXFIT tagság az, ami utána is
viszi tovább." names the outcome (continuity) rather than a feature list (video count, program
count, etc.).

**"Price-per-use" framing** did not turn up a measured source in this pass — flagged as a gap,
not fabricated. The directionally-relevant adjacent evidence is the guarantee/risk-reversal
literature (§3), which functions similarly: it converts an abstract commitment into something
concretely bounded (a number of workouts, a number of weeks, a number of forints).

**Naming what continues vs. what begins** is the single cleanest, most directly-applicable
finding in this section for LEXFIT specifically, because it maps onto a documented example: the
Copyhackers audit's Userbrain example frames its guarantee as ongoing partnership language
("you'll get a new one at no extra cost") rather than a one-time transaction promise. LEXFIT's
rail lead is structurally identical — "the weekly plan is yours; membership is what carries it
forward" is a continuation frame, not a "join now" frame. This is worth protecting explicitly
as a page-wide principle, not just a one-line accident: the CTA, the guarantee framing, and the
exit line should all use continuation vocabulary (folytat-, visz tovább-, marad) rather than
beginning vocabulary (csatlakozz, kezdd el, indulj), because the reveal page's entire premise is
"you already did the hard part; membership just keeps it going."

**Self-efficacy and process-vs-outcome praise, for a past-failure audience**:
Bandura's self-efficacy theory identifies four sources of belief in one's own capability —
mastery experience (strongest), vicarious experience, verbal persuasion, and interpretation of
physiological state — and specifically documents that people with *high* self-efficacy reframe
setbacks as "insufficient effort or poor strategy," which are changeable, rather than fixed
inability, which isn't
([simplypsychology.org](https://www.simplypsychology.org/self-efficacy.html);
[en.wikipedia.org/Self-efficacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-efficacy)). Copy that
implicitly assigns fixed blame ("you never stick with anything") or that promises a fixed
outcome ("this time you'll succeed") both work against this — the first triggers the fixed-
inability read directly, the second sets up outcome-praise dynamics that Dweck's growth-mindset
research found undermine resilience after a future setback (praising ability/outcome makes
failure feel identity-threatening; praising process protects motivation through failure —
[Dweck's *Mindset*, via Wikipedia synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mindset_(book))).
**Copy implication**: keep the commitment-prompt hint's *process* framing ("don't pick your best
day, pick an average one" — this is literally advice about approach/strategy, not a promise of
outcome) and avoid adding any line that promises a result ("ezúttal sikerül," "ezúttal menni
fog") anywhere on the page, including in the guarantee framing.

## 3. Guarantee copywriting

Real, verified examples pulled directly from live subscription/fitness pricing pages:

| Brand | Verbatim guarantee/trial copy | Structure |
|---|---|---|
| [Future.co](https://www.future.co/) | "Cancel within 30 days for a full refund." | Condition first, payoff second, one sentence, concrete number (30 days) |
| [Basecamp](https://basecamp.com/pricing) | "Yes, you can cancel any time, no questions asked, no forms to fill out, no person to talk to." | Unconditional, negation-stacked ("no X, no Y, no Z") to preempt specific frictions |
| [Kit / ConvertKit](https://kit.com/pricing) | "No credit card required. Cancel anytime." | Two independent unconditional claims, no guarantee per se — risk removed before it exists |
| [Peloton App](https://www.onepeloton.com/app) | "New members try for free. Cancel anytime. ... Terms apply." | Trial-based risk reversal, "terms apply" pushed to the edge, not the headline |
| [Noom](https://www.noom.com/plans) | "7-day trial available, cancel anytime" (in-line with the price) | Folded directly into the price line, not a separate guarantee block |
| [Userbrain, via Copyhackers audit](https://copyhackers.com/2025/03/saas-pricing-page-checklist/) | "If you're not happy with one of our testers, you'll get a new one at no extra cost." | Condition-first, names the specific dissatisfaction, resolves with a concrete remedy (not just "refund") |

Patterns across all six: **every one is condition-first or unconditional-and-short**; none
lead with an abstract concept sentence the way LEXFIT's current guarantee framing does. None use
the word "guarantee" as a noun standing alone — they state the mechanism (cancel, refund, no
questions) rather than the label. **Numbers are load-bearing**: 30 days, 7 days, "no extra cost"
— vague "money back" doesn't appear in any pulled example; every real guarantee is either a
concrete number or an unconditional negation.

**Condition-first vs. promise-first**: the examples split roughly evenly on which comes first
(Future.co is condition→payoff; Basecamp and Kit are promise→then the specifics), but every
single one states the *mechanism* early rather than an abstract frame. LEXFIT's current line —
„Nem fogadás. Egy útvonal, aminek a végén te döntesz." (not a bet — a route where you decide at
the end) — is a *frame*, not a mechanism. It's a legitimate rhetorical move (see "permission
slip" below) but it cannot be the whole guarantee; it needs a condition-first, numeric sentence
doing the actual work, with the frame line as a companion, not a replacement.

**"Permission slip" framing**: the closest documented pattern is Userbrain's — the guarantee
removes the pressure to have gotten it right the first time ("you'll get a new one," not "we'll
refund you if we failed you"). Applied to LEXFIT's actual mechanic (10 workouts in 5 weeks, full
refund), a permission-slip version would explicitly say the person doesn't have to trust the
outcome in advance — they just have to do the thing, then decide. This is close to what LEXFIT's
existing frame line is reaching for ("...aminek a végén te döntesz" already defers the decision
to the end) — the frame line is good raw material, it's just currently unpaired with the
condition-first mechanism sentence.

**"Reason why" literature**: classic direct-response doctrine (Claude Hopkins → Gary
Bencivenga's "reason why" school) holds that stating *why* an offer exists increases believability
over the offer alone. I could not retrieve a live primary source for this in-session (searches
exhausted, targeted fetches to Copyblogger/CXL 403'd or 404'd) — flagging as practitioner
consensus, not measured evidence, and not citable with a working URL from this pass.

## 4. Micro-copy at fields/buttons that precede payment

**"Cancel anytime" is near-universal** in the real examples pulled (§3: Basecamp, Kit, Peloton,
Noom all carry it, independent of whether they also have a numeric guarantee). Its consistent
placement is directly under or beside the primary CTA, not buried in FAQ or terms — it functions
as an immediate-proximity trust signal at the exact moment of the ask, not a separate reassurance
elsewhere on the page. I did not find a controlled-test source quantifying its specific lift (the
Nielsen Norman Group and Baymard pages I targeted for this either 404'd or 403'd this session) —
its evidentiary status here is "near-universal real-world practice," not "measured lift," and
should be labeled that way.

**Naming the next step on the button vs. a generic label**: no controlled study surfaced this
pass. The stage-matching literature in §1 (bima.co.uk) is the closest adjacent evidence —
buttons that accurately describe the actual next screen out-perform generic ones because they
resolve "what happens when I click" uncertainty, which is the same anxiety class as "what happens
when I click" microcopy is meant to solve. **Copy implication**: whatever CTA verb LEXFIT lands
on, the microcopy immediately below it should say, in one short clause, what screen comes next
(e.g., naming that the price/options appear next, not payment) — this is a plain application of
reducing click-anxiety rather than a swap of the CTA's own verb.

## 5. The quiet register as a conversion strategy

Direct measured evidence specific to "understatement beats hype for skeptical audiences" did not
surface in this pass — every targeted fetch toward B2B tone-of-voice research (Demand Curve, CXL
messaging-strategy pages) 403'd or 404'd, and the general web-search budget was exhausted before
this question could get dedicated queries. What's available:

- **Damaging admission / "reason why" schools** (Blair Warren's widely-reproduced "one sentence
  persuasion" heuristic — people respond to copy that "confirms their suspicions" and "allays
  their fears" rather than copy that oversells) is an extremely widely-cited practitioner
  concept, not a controlled study. I located the concept's presence on CXL's site via a redirect
  chain ([cxl.com/blog/one-sentence-persuasion/](https://cxl.com/blog/one-sentence-persuasion/))
  but the page 403'd on fetch, so the quote here is from training knowledge, not a page I read
  this session — flagged accordingly, not presented as freshly verified.
- **The self-efficacy and process-praise findings in §2** indirectly support quiet copy for this
  specific audience: language that avoids promising an outcome and avoids implying fixed
  inability is, structurally, quieter language than hype copy (which trades in outcome promises
  and urgency).
- **Where quiet copy fails**: the one concrete risk this research did surface is in LEXFIT's own
  current rail heading (§8) — a conditional/hypothetical sentence structure ("Ha rendszert
  csinálnál belőle" — "if you'd make a system of it") can cross from "calm" into "vague" when the
  conditional mood adds psychological distance at exactly the moment the page wants the reader
  to feel continuity, not hypotheticals. This is inference from the continuation-framing
  principle in §2, not a separately-sourced claim.

## 6. Personalized echo in copy

**General personalization lift**: 80% of surveyed consumers report being more likely to do
business with a company that personalizes, 90% find personalization appealing, and consumers who
rate personalization as "very appealing" are reported as 10x more likely to be a brand's most
valuable customers (Epsilon/GBH Insights 2017 survey, n=1,000 US consumers 18–64, cross-validated
against Epsilon's Abacus transactional cooperative —
[epsilon.com](https://www.epsilon.com/us/about-us/pressroom/new-epsilon-research-indicates-80-of-consumers-are-more-likely-to-make-a-purchase-when-brands-offer-personalized-experiences)).
This is real, measured-adjacent (survey + transactional cross-validation), but it is general
marketing personalization, not a study of quiz-based plan-reveal pages specifically — treat the
number as directional support, not proof this exact tactic works at this exact page.

**Applying it to the reveal page**: LEXFIT already knows the user's chosen workout days from the
onboarding quiz (per repo context: cadence/weekday selection feeds the plan). None of the decision-
page copy currently echoes this back — the commitment prompt asks „Melyik nap kezded?" as a fresh
open question rather than referencing the days the user already picked in onboarding. This is a
clear, low-risk personalization opportunity: echoing the user's own prior answer ("hétfő, mint
amit korábban választottál" — "Monday, like you picked before") is the safe end of personalization
because it repeats information the user gave voluntarily and expects the system to remember, as
opposed to inferring something they didn't explicitly state.

**The creepiness boundary**: no measured source surfaced distinguishing the line precisely. The
generally-accepted practitioner boundary (not sourced live this session) is: echoing what the
user *explicitly told you* reads as attentive; echoing what the system *inferred* about them
(especially anything body/health-adjacent) reads as surveillance. For LEXFIT this maps cleanly
onto the existing hard rule against "second-person health assumptions" — echo the day-of-week and
count answers (explicit, neutral, low-stakes) and do not echo anything inferred about the user's
body, motivation type, or past failures, even if the onboarding data would technically allow it.

## 7. Hungarian-specific patterns

This is the thinnest-evidenced section, and it should be read that way. Hungarian-language
direct-response/e-commerce copywriting research is not well represented in generally-fetchable
English-language sources, and the web-search budget was exhausted before dedicated Hungarian-
market queries could run. What follows is one verified legal fact plus explicit inference from
adjacent CEE/DACH markets — none of it should be treated as measured Hungarian-specific evidence.

- **Verified**: the EU Consumer Rights Directive (2011/83/EU) establishes a 14-day right of
  withdrawal for distance/online purchases across all EU member states including Hungary
  ([en.wikipedia.org/Consumer_Rights_Directive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consumer_Rights_Directive)).
  Hungarian consumers are accustomed to seeing "elállási jog" named explicitly on checkout/pricing
  pages of legitimate Hungarian webshops — its absence is more likely to read as a red flag to a
  skeptical, 35–54-year-old buyer than its presence reads as boilerplate. **Copy implication**:
  the guarantee section (or its immediate FAQ neighbor) should name "elállási jog" explicitly
  alongside the 10-workout/5-week mechanism, not rely on the guarantee alone to imply legal
  compliance — the phrase itself is a trust signal independent of the guarantee's generosity.
- **Inference, not sourced**: tegeződés at the money moment is already standard in Hungarian D2C/
  SaaS (most Hungarian subscription products tegez by the pricing page even if they magáz earlier
  in a funnel) — LEXFIT's existing tegeződő register at this stage is consistent with category
  norms, not a risk.
  Similarly, Ft-formatting conventions (space as thousands separator, "Ft" suffix not prefix,
  e.g. "5 990 Ft" not "Ft5,990") are near-universal in Hungarian retail and should be treated as
  a hygiene requirement rather than a persuasion lever.
- **Known cultural skepticism triggers** (inference from CEE-market commentary, not a specific
  study): Hungarian buyers in this age band are generally reported by practitioners as more
  skeptical of superlative/hype language than US audiences and more reassured by concrete,
  checkable mechanisms (exact price, exact refund path, named legal right) than by emotive
  copy — which aligns with, but does not independently confirm, the brand's existing no-hype
  constraint.

## 8. Headline evaluation

Evaluated against the evidence above. "Strong" = keep as primary; "Moderate" = defensible but
worth A/B testing against an alternative; each line gets 2–3 Hungarian alternatives with the
specific principle each one applies. All alternatives respect every hard rule (no exclamation
marks, no urgency, no weight-loss vocabulary, no second-person health assumptions, no invented
statistics/testimonials, tegeződés).

**Headline — „A heted, készen"** — **Strong.** Already first-person-possessive by construction
("heted" = "your week" via possessive suffix) and states a completed deliverable, which is the
right frame for a reveal moment (§2, continuation/ownership framing).
- Alt A (personalized echo, §6): „A heted készen — {N} napra, ahogy megadtad" — names the day-
  count the user chose, applying the Epsilon-adjacent personalization finding directly.
- Alt B (two-beat, quieter): „Ez a heted. Készen." — splits possession and completion into two
  short declaratives; tests whether the shorter cadence reads as calmer or as clipped/cold.

**Rail heading — „Ha rendszert csinálnál belőle"** — **Moderate, worth testing against an
alternative.** The conditional mood ("ha... csinálnál") introduces psychological distance right
where the page's own rail lead is trying to establish continuity (§2, §5) — it risks reading as
hypothetical rather than as a natural next step from a plan the reader already has in hand.
- Alt A (continuity verb, matches rail lead's "viszi tovább"): „Ha ezt viszed tovább" — keeps the
  conditional "ha" but ties the verb to the same continuation vocabulary as the sentence below it,
  removing the vocabulary mismatch.
- Alt B (presupposition close, still no urgency): „Amikor rendszer lesz belőle" — swaps "if" for
  "when," presupposing continuation without setting a deadline; a mild register shift worth
  testing since it's a bigger swing than Alt A.
- Alt C: „Ha innen folytatod" — most literal application of "name what continues" (§2), pairs
  "innen" (from here) with "folytatod" (you continue) to anchor it explicitly to the plan just
  shown.

**Rail lead — „A heti terved a tiéd. A LEXFIT tagság az, ami utána is viszi tovább."** —
**Strong, no change recommended.** This is the cleanest application of the "name what continues,
not what begins" principle found anywhere on the page (§2) — keep it as the anchor and make sure
the CTA and guarantee framing are edited to match its vocabulary rather than the reverse.

**CTA — „Megnézem a tagságot"** — **Moderate — defensible, but the single highest-value test on
the page.** It's correctly first-person (§1) but it's an inspection verb at a moment that is
functionally past "explore" (§1). Worth a direct A/B against a continuation-flavored verb, since
the rail lead has already reframed membership as continuation rather than a new commitment.
- Alt A (continuation verb, matches rail lead directly): „Folytatom a tagsággal" — "I continue
  with the membership" — first-person, no exclamation, names continuation not a fresh start.
- Alt B (slightly more decisive, still process-not-outcome): „Viszem tovább" — "I carry it
  forward" — shortest option, echoes "viszi tovább" from the rail lead almost verbatim, high
  vocabulary consistency.
- Alt C (control — keep current, but only if the next screen is genuinely a neutral options view
  rather than checkout): „Megnézem a tagságot" stays valid *if* paired with next-step microcopy
  (§4) that removes the "what happens when I click" uncertainty an inspection verb otherwise
  leaves open.

**Exit line — „Ha most nem időszerű: a heti terved akkor is a tiéd marad."** — **Strong, no
change recommended.** Textbook permission-slip reassurance (§3) with no pressure and no loss-
aversion manipulation — respects autonomy in a way the self-efficacy literature (§2) says matters
for a repeat-failure audience specifically.
- Alt A (only if testing appetite exists): „Semmi nem vész el, ha most nem lépsz." — more directly
  names the reassurance (nothing is lost) rather than restating ownership; slightly higher risk of
  reading as loss-aversion-adjacent despite the calm phrasing, so treat as a test variant, not a
  default improvement.

**Guarantee framing — „Nem fogadás. Egy útvonal, aminek a végén te döntesz."** — **Moderate — good
raw material, incomplete as-is.** Every real guarantee pulled in §3 is condition-first and
numeric; this line is a frame/payoff with no mechanism attached. It should stay, but as a
companion sentence to a condition-first numeric sentence, not as the guarantee's only copy.
- Alt A (condition-first companion, states the actual mechanism per §3's pattern): „Ha 5 hét
  alatt teljesíted a 10 edzést, és mégsem érzed jól, minden forintot visszakapsz." — pair this
  directly before or after the existing frame line.
- Alt B (permission-slip framing per §3's Userbrain pattern): „Nem kell előre hinned benne.
  Csináld végig, aztán döntesz." — removes the pressure to have faith upfront, which is the
  specific self-efficacy risk this audience carries (§2).
- Alt C (combined, if the page has room for two sentences here): „Ha 5 hét alatt teljesíted a 10
  edzést és mégsem érzed jól, minden forintot visszakapsz. Nem fogadás — egy útvonal, aminek a
  végén te döntesz." — condition-first mechanism, then the existing frame line as payoff, matching
  the structure of every real example in §3.

**Commitment prompt — „Melyik nap kezded?" / hint „Ne a legjobb napodra időzítsd. Egy
átlagosra."** — **Strong — the best-evidenced line on the page.** This is a near-textbook
implementation-intention prompt (when/where planning) paired with explicit effort/strategy
framing rather than outcome framing, which is exactly what the self-efficacy and process-praise
research (§2) says helps a repeat-failure audience specifically. No change recommended to the
mechanism; the only upgrade available is personalization (§6).
- Alt A (personalized echo, only if plan days are known at this point): „Melyik nap kezded — a
  {nap1}, a {nap2} vagy a {nap3} közül?" — lists the user's own previously-chosen days rather than
  asking as a fully open question; low creepiness risk since it echoes an explicit prior answer
  (§6), not an inference.
